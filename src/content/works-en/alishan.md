  - type: narrative
    id: partnership-roles
    title: What should the center teach, and what should the tribe teach?
    markdown: |-
      The project began because the center needed to develop courses related to Indigenous peoples. Before arranging any activity, I first faced one question: what should the center's teachers teach? And who should explain the tribe's own culture and knowledge of the mountains and forest?

      I did not think it was right for center teachers to look up information, learn a few activities, and teach the tribe's own culture in place of tribal teachers. But if we only bought a ready-made experience tour, the center's own environmental education and learning guidance would not really be part of it.

      Through my supervisor, we got in touch with a tribe and learned about the culture and mountain life that its teachers were already teaching. Step by step, I arranged the partnership as: "We teach what we should teach, and they teach what they should teach."

      The center is in charge of the learning before going into the mountains, such as preparation, respect for culture, and environmental ethics. Tribal teachers lead their own culture, the local environment, and hands-on life in the mountains and forest. I was in charge of the overall plan, how the lessons connect, and the contact with partners. I also managed recruiting, the budget, transport, communication with parents, and running the camp on site.
  - type: narrative

    title: Get ready first, then go into the tribe
    markdown: |-
      The center and the tribe are far enough apart that travel is needed. With only a one-day round trip, travel would take up a lot of time, and it would be hard to do learning that needs an overnight stay and hands-on work. So the plan slowly became three days and two nights: get ready at the center first, then tribal teachers lead the experience that follows. Transport, cost, and learning time were planned together.

  - type: narrative
    id: prelude-courses
    markdown: |-
      ### On Day one, there is more to prepare than gear

      I planned the overall direction of four lessons before the camp and how they connect. I designed Hunting Miracle. The other three were mainly developed and taught by colleagues at the center:

      - **Gear and safety:** preparing gear and safety before going into the mountains.
      - **Understanding culture:** cultural stereotypes, respect, and manners for learning.
      - **Hunting today:** modern hunting, commercial hunting, and related laws.
      - **Hunting Miracle:** exploring cooperation, dividing work, limited resources, and environmental ethics through a simulation game.

      When we talked about cultural stereotypes, a student once asked: "Can they speak Mandarin?" Questions like this made the need for the lessons before the camp clear: before going into the tribe, students may still hold many ideas they have never looked at.

      A tribal teacher once gave feedback that students who went through the center's lessons before the camp were better prepared, and the teaching on site could go into deeper discussion.

      Hunting Miracle is one part of this preparation. For how the materials and the game were designed, see the [separate case](/en/works/hunting/) for it.

  - type: narrative
    id: field-learning
    markdown: |-
      ### Giving the learning about culture and the mountain forest back to the tribe

      On Days two and three, tribal teachers lead the learning about the local environment, culture, houses, tools, and life in the mountains and forest. At one point, the activities included gathering mountain palm, and using its leaves and bamboo to build a hunting hut where students stayed that night. Depending on the year and the site, there were also night activities, fish traps, and stream work.

      Center teachers go with the students, help with safety and with connecting the learning, and lead the wrap-up on site at the end. This lets students look back at how the lessons before the camp relate to the real experience.

  - type: narrative
    id: operations
    title: Running the camp within limits on group size and transport
    markdown: |-
      ### Seven students: rearranging teachers and transport

      For a regular camp, a medium-size bus is arranged based on the number of students, and about three center teachers go to the tribe. One camp had only seven students. Using the same vehicle and staff would not have fit that size.

      I changed the center teachers to two: one went with the seven students in a nine-seat vehicle, and the other rode a motorcycle. Transport, staff, and the way of travel were adjusted together, and this camp still went ahead as usual.

      When working with the tribe, we followed the existing course fees. We did not keep costs down by lowering the fees for tribal teachers. Instead, we adjusted transport and other items that could be changed.

      ### Connecting pick-up and on-site arrangements

      Station pick-up was once changed from a bus to several taxis. On the last day, we used the vehicle that was already going down the mountain. It stopped briefly at the center and then went straight to the station, so no separate shuttle was needed. The wrap-up for learning was already done in the tribe, so the stop on the way did not need another lesson.

      Before the trip, we collected information on meals, allergies, and special needs, and arranged insurance and teachers and tribal staff to go with the group. Life jackets are used for stream activities, and there is an emergency medical process and incident record on site. These preparations were planned together with the lessons, lodging, and transport, so each part works with the others.
  - type: narrative
    id: site-change
    title: The partner venue changed suddenly, and the camp was rearranged
    markdown: |-
      Once, the original partner tribe could not hold the camp as planned because the venue and the conditions for running it changed. They helped introduce a second tribe. I contacted the second tribe right away and visited the site. We talked about the activities and venue conditions they could offer, and then I rearranged the schedule, transport, and safety matters, and told participants about the change of venue.

      This change was not just moving the original schedule to another tribe. The core activities could mostly be kept, but the venue conditions, how people move between activities, and the teaching plan all had to be checked again. I went through these one by one with the second tribe. I kept the original learning goals, and put the schedule together again to fit the local site and teaching content.

      The second tribe also offered different hands-on work. For example, a tribal teacher led students to make their own eating tools from bamboo, which they then used at meals over the next two days. This is teaching content that the second tribe provided, and I was in charge of fitting it into the whole schedule.

      It took about two to three days to arrange the replacement plan, and the camp was finally run with the adjusted schedule. The venue changed, but the lessons at the center, the learning led by tribal teachers, and the wrap-up on site were all kept.
  - type: narrative
    id: continuing-program
    title: From a trial to a program that keeps running
    markdown: |-
      When the trial began in about 2021, the pandemic had an effect. Tribal hunters and teachers once came to the center to run the activities, which was different from the later version where students stay in the tribe. After that, the three-day, two-night structure slowly took shape: Day one at the center, and Days two and three in the tribe.

      As the camp ran each year, the team kept adjusting the activities, their order, and transport, and also got used to how each other works. Hands-on work such as fish traps, and the pick-up arrangements, were added or fixed little by little while the camp was running.

      I also started a LINE group for parents on my own, to give information before the trip, reminders about gear, and a way to reach us. During the camp, after the main activities, we shared a few photos and short notes, so parents knew what their children were doing. Parents once said this made them feel more at ease and involved. Later, other camps also used a similar way.

      Later, some students joined again and brought their siblings. Some parents signed up by themselves after their child took part, and a school teacher booked an extra camp. The lessons, the division of work, and the administrative steps built up year by year. I am still the main contact for partners, and I keep adjusting the arrangements for each camp. From a trial of the lessons, to a camp that can be adjusted for different numbers of students and different partnership conditions, this camp slowly became an education program that the center can run again and again, and not just a one-time activity.
