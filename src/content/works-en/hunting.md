  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-4
    title: At first, even staying alive was hard
    markdown: |-
      Hunting Miracle came from a need for introductory teaching on the first day of the Alishan Hunter Growth Camp. I was mainly in charge of the game mechanics, rules, and game materials. I wanted students, before they enter the tribe, to understand cooperation, limited resources, and environmental ethics through their own choices and results.

      Players form groups and play members of a community that lives on forest resources. They need to get food and share resources to keep the community alive. But hunting is affected by tools, probability, and a limited number of actions. Trying again and again does not always bring success.

      In early teaching, there were players who hunted on their own, did not have enough food, and were out of the game quickly. How to get enough food, and how to share it, became a problem players had to face from the start.
  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-2
    title: When players began to cooperate, things changed
    markdown: |-
      In the game, different hunting tools have different chances of success, and a player's skill also affects the result. I linked rules such as hunting probability, food needs, limits on actions, and forest resources, so that tool use, food sharing, and hunting results affect each other. This creates a game setting where it is hard for the community to keep surviving through individual action alone.

      Using only your own tool, and trading tools so that more skilled players do the hunting, can bring different food results. Food can also be shared. Players need to think about who has enough food, who needs support, and how to divide resources between surviving now and improving future hunting ability. In real teaching, there were also players who began to trade tools, share food, and arrange roles to try to keep more members alive. These choices change who uses the tools, how food is shared, and who can stay in the game.

      When teaching, I first make the shared goal of surviving together clear, but I do not directly teach players how to trade tools, share food, or arrange roles. I leave these specific strategies for players to find through trying, results, and discussion. I want players to understand, from the survival needs and results in the game, why cooperation is a reasonable and important choice, and not only because the teacher told them "cooperation is important."

      In development, I used Excel to organize how the success chances of different tools, the number of players, and the amount of forest resources relate to each other, to estimate and adjust the difficulty of the game. I wanted players to feel the pressure of survival when hunting alone, while cooperation and role sharing give more chances, and luck and resource limits are still there.

  - type: narrative
    id: forest-information
    title: Just when they learned to hunt, the forest was empty
    markdown: |-
      Cooperation gave players more ways to get food. But once they became more efficient, another problem appeared.

      One time, a group of students began to cooperate earlier than I expected. They quickly set up roles, and their hunting got much more efficient. As a result, they hunted all the animals in the forest, and the game ended right away.

      I did not expect this result to come so soon. It changed the question from "How can I catch something?" to "Can we keep hunting?" Cooperation solved the problem of getting food, but it did not automatically solve the problem of keeping resources over a long time.

      A successful hunt uses up animals in the forest, and the forest recovers based on the resources that remain. When players catch too many, fewer animals are left, and the number that can recover later also goes down. More food now may make it harder to keep going in the next round.

      Players cannot know exactly how many resources are left in the forest. If they want to know more, they can give up one hunting chance in exchange for a rough clue about the forest. What they get is still incomplete information.

      So besides thinking about "how to get food," players must also decide whether to hunt one time less, to learn about the forest first. They have to weigh the need for food now against getting information.

  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-6
    title: Make the game hard, but not so hard that players cannot keep playing
    markdown: |-
      An early version that was too complicated was not adopted. The way of playing also kept being changed. The hunting result changed from drawing lots to a dice roll, and the game materials changed from white paper and cards to harder cards that last longer, which are easier to recognize on site and to use again.

      Excel can help estimate the difficulty, but real trial plays are still needed. I asked colleagues to help test. Some tried to find gaps in the rules, so I could check the limits and possible results, and keep adjusting. Some trade and sharing strategies that looked like "using loopholes" were kept so that players could try them.

      In real teaching, another problem became clear. With too few hints at the start, players might treat the game as a competition, each chasing their own hunting results, and the whole group would quickly die out from lack of food. I also needed to let players keep trying when early hunting did not go well.

  - type: narrative
    id: player-strategies
    markdown: |-
      The starting dried meat is my change to the starting conditions. During the game, players also suggested processing extra meat to keep it for later use. This is a strategy that is allowed, but it costs actions, so players still need to weigh keeping food against other actions.

      These changes did not turn the game into one that guarantees a win. The dice still bring uncertainty, and cooperation is still limited by forest resources. But the changes to the guidance and the starting resources give players a chance to understand the problem while playing, and then try different ways.
  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-5
    title: When the game ends, the real discussion begins
    markdown: |-
      After the game ends, I do not start explaining right away. First I ask each group to look back: "What did you do to keep the tribe alive?"

      Players can start from the choices they just made. Why did they choose to cooperate or change strategy? Which actions affected other members? Why did the forest resources change? If they played again, what would they change? The discussion starts from what actually happened. If an important idea does not come up by itself, I bring it in with guiding questions and extra explanation.

      While waiting for the dice result, some participants prayed, blew on the dice, or asked for good luck. These natural reactions also became material for looking back on uncertainty and the survival of the group.

      I am in charge of this simulation and discussion, so that students first go through choices and results under limited resources. After entering the tribe, culture, life in the mountains and forest, and real hunting experience are still led by tribal teachers.

      Hunting Miracle is already used in the lessons before the camp, and it keeps being adjusted through trial plays and real use. To see how this learning connects to the experiences that follow, you can next read about the [Alishan Hunter Growth Camp](/en/works/alishan/) case.
