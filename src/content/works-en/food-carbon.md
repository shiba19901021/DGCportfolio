  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-1
    title: From a cooking activity, we found a need for choosing again
    markdown: |-
      Food Carbon Detective did not start from carbon footprints. It started from how to cook a meal. Early outdoor courses used ways of cooking such as roast chicken in a cardboard box and rocket stoves made from milk powder cans. Later, as stove building and [how the Eco Kitchen was built](/en/works/eco-kitchen/) went on, cooking activities slowly added food and farming education and food carbon footprints.

      I once used the carbon footprint worksheet from "Xue Senlin" (學森林) as a reference. But after using it, I felt that just choosing food from set options still gave limited interaction, and it was not close enough to daily life. So I started from "What dish do you want to cook today?" Students first imagine a dish, then choose ingredients under different conditions. This links the abstract idea of a food carbon footprint to daily eating.

      The aim of the course is also not to have students calculate exact carbon emissions of ingredients. For the same ingredient, the real carbon footprint can be very different depending on production, transport, and storage. I wanted students to first understand that different ingredient choices may have different environmental impacts, and then take this information into daily decisions.

      When I redesigned the "Gourmet Small Kitchen / Carbon Footprint Game," I put the two rounds of choosing ingredients into the core of the game. After that, I kept adding ingredients, organizing information, and adjusting the teaching tools, and step by step it became today's Food Carbon Detective.
  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-3
    title: Choose once, then bring in the carbon footprint information
    markdown: |-
      The most important design in the whole game is the order in which information appears. In real life, food choices often start from taste, habit, price, or "what I want to eat." I let students start from these familiar conditions and make their own dish choices. Then I bring in the environmental information that they had not considered.
  - type: timeline
    id: two-choices
    steps:
      - title: First choice | Put together the dish they want to cook
        text: Students play cooks in groups and choose ingredients by the information they can see, such as price, nutrition, taste, and origin. The carbon footprint levels of the ingredients are not shown yet.
      - title: New information | See the journey food makes before it reaches the table
        text: The teacher shows the relative carbon levels of the ingredients made for teaching, and explains how production, transport, and environmental impact are related. Students go back and compare the ingredients they chose, and talk about why different choices give different results.
      - title: Second choice | Put environmental impact into the judgment
        text: Students compare the ingredients again. Besides price, taste, and nutrition, they add environmental impact, decide which ingredients to keep or replace, and explain what they thought about.
  - type: narrative
    id: comparing-information
    markdown: |-
      Only now does the carbon footprint information relate to the decisions students just made. Where does the food come from? What effects may come from local or imported food and from different ways of producing it? If they want to lower the environmental impact of this dish, what are they willing to change?

      The second choice keeps room for discussion. Students can compare different conditions and explain why they changed an ingredient, or they can keep their first choice. The point is to give them a chance to judge again with new information, and not to hand them one correct menu from the teacher.

  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-7
    title: With more ingredients, the teaching tools had to change too
    markdown: |-
      The early version had about 50 kinds of ingredients. To give more ways to put dishes together and compare, I slowly added more, up to about 100, and organized the information for each ingredient again.

      Based on how each ingredient is produced and transported and other related environmental factors, I designed relative carbon levels from 1 to 10 for teaching, so students can compare when they choose. This is a comparison model made for teaching. It is not an exact measurement of the carbon emissions of each ingredient.

      As the data grew, the loose cards became less convenient to handle, read, and keep. So later the ingredient information was put into a handbook, to make the materials easier to read and to use again.

  - type: narrative
    id: original-section-5
    title: From choosing ingredients in a simulation to real cooking and cycling
    markdown: |-
      The course does not stop at the table game. In the full one-day course, students learn about and gather local ingredients, and then go into the Eco Kitchen to prepare ingredients, share the work, and cook over a wood fire. Food that was only compared in the materials becomes material in their hands that must be washed, cut, and cooked. Pruned branches from the site also become fuel they can use.

      The real cooking is arranged by season, buying, materials, people, and safety conditions. It does not buy everything on the menu chosen in the game. The game lets students practice comparing, and the cooking lets them meet the real materials and limits on site.

      After the meal, the learning continues to washing and cleaning up: raw food waste goes into compost, and gray water from washing goes to plants. Students follow things from where the ingredients come from, to cooking and energy use, to where things go after use. Then they go back to talk about how these practices relate to daily life.

    id: course-use-and-sharing
    markdown: |-
      The course is listed in the [official course introduction on Taiwan Forest Recreation (山林悠遊網)](https://recreation.forest.gov.tw/Education/Course?id=CK-A21516). Colleagues and the teaching team prepare the materials, lead groups, and teach on site together, and then keep adjusting based on how the course is used. Some partner organizations have also come back with different students. The arrangements are also adjusted for each group's age, needs, time, and conditions on site.

      Over many sessions, I saw that after students get the new information, they think again about where ingredients come from and their environmental impact, and the discussion moves from "what do I want to eat" to "why did I choose this." Teachers and volunteers who helped also once said that the form of game plus cooking makes abstract ideas easier to bring into class discussion.

      ### Writing up the practice as an English presentation

      On 13 September 2026, I shared the practical experience of this course in the 11:00 online session in English at the 37th Annual Meeting of the Japanese Society for Environmental Education (JSFEE). The abstract number is 37F07. The official title is:

      **Learning Low-Carbon Living Through Play: A Practice Report on the "Food Carbon Detective" Program at Chukou Nature Education Center, Taiwan**

      While preparing the presentation, I sorted out again how the two rounds of choosing ingredients, the ingredient information, and the hands-on cooking relate to each other. I also put my many years of experience on site into a form that I could explain to environmental educators from different backgrounds. You can read the [official English abstract (37F07)](https://www.jsfee.jp/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/JSFEE_37th_abstract_english.pdf) and the [official conference program](https://www.jsfee.jp/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/jsfee37_ver.2_6_260911.pdf) for details.

      The exchange also raised questions about living costs and site conditions: How can different families afford this? What can be done when an urban school does not have the same kitchen and land? My answer started from the resources each place can get: ingredients can be found from a nearby market first, and without a large piece of land, it can still start with herbs or small-scale growing. The course can keep its core of comparing and choosing, and then adjust to each place's life and site conditions.
